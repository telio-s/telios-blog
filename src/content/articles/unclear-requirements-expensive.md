Most software projects do not become expensive only because developers write code slowly. They become expensive because people start building before the important decisions are clear.

A vague requirement can sound harmless at first. "We need a dashboard." "Customers should be able to request service." "The admin can approve orders." These statements are useful starting points, but they are not enough for a team to estimate, build, and verify the work reliably.

The expensive part appears later. A workflow has an exception. A role needs permission that was never discussed. A screen looks correct but does not match how staff actually work. The team then has to decide whether the change is a bug, a missing requirement, or a new request.

Good requirements are not long documents for their own sake. They create shared understanding. They explain the business goal, the user roles, the workflow, the business rules, and the acceptance criteria.

When the business side and technical side can point to the same agreement, the conversation becomes calmer. Estimation is clearer. Change requests are easier to identify. Delivery can be reviewed against something concrete.

That clarity is where a project saves money.
